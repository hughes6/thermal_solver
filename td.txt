THERMAL DESKTOP VALIDATION MODEL
================================

Purpose
-------
Use this model to validate the solver's transient energy conservation and
solid-temperature response for the minimal rack case.

This is a sealed transient model, not a steady-state model. With a 1000 W heat
source and no fan, vent, or external heat sink, the temperature must continue
to rise. A steady solution is physically impossible.


Geometry
--------
Rack:
  Width:  0.5 m
  Depth:  0.5 m
  Height: 1.0 m
  Origin: (0.0, 0.0, 0.0) m

Aluminum block:
  Width:  0.5 m
  Depth:  0.5 m
  Height: 0.2 m
  Origin: (0.0, 0.0, 0.4) m
  Heat load: 1000 W total

Because the block spans the complete rack width and depth, it divides the rack
air into two separate volumes:

  Lower air volume: 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.4 m
  Upper air volume: 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.4 m


Thermal Desktop setup
---------------------
1. Create the aluminum block as a finite-difference or finite-element solid.

2. Assign the same aluminum properties used by the thermal-solver TOML:

     Density:         2700 kg/m^3
     Specific heat:    900 J/kg-K
     Conductivity:      205 W/m-K, or the exact TOML value

3. Apply 1000 W to the complete aluminum solid. This is a total heat load, not
   a volumetric value of 1000 W/m^3.

4. Set the initial temperature of the block and air to 20 degC, or exactly the
   same initial temperature used in the thermal-solver model.

5. Leave the rack exterior adiabatic for the basic energy-conservation test.
   Do not apply an ambient sink unless the identical boundary condition is
   added to the thermal-solver model.

6. For a conduction-only comparison, represent the upper and lower air spaces
   using air material volumes and connect their faces to the aluminum block.

7. For a natural-convection comparison, use FloCAD or a documented convection
   correlation. A lumped convection network will not reproduce a complete 3-D
   buoyant CFD field, so compare bulk temperatures and heat balances before
   comparing local temperatures.

8. Use approximately the same spatial resolution as the thermal-solver case.
   Perform a mesh-refinement comparison in both programs before comparing
   localized maximum temperatures.

9. Run a transient solution for 600 seconds with results saved every 10 seconds.


Analytical energy benchmark
---------------------------
Aluminum volume:

  V = 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.2 = 0.05 m^3

Aluminum mass:

  m = 0.05 x 2700 = 135 kg

Aluminum thermal capacitance:

  C = 135 x 900 = 121,500 J/K

For an adiabatic block with a uniform 1000 W load:

  dT/dt = 1000 / 121,500
        = 0.008230 K/s
        = 0.4938 K/min
        = 29.63 K/hour

After 600 seconds:

  delta T = 4.938 degC
  average block temperature = approximately 24.938 degC

The air adds a small amount of thermal capacitance. For 0.20 m^3 of air at
rho = 1.225 kg/m^3 and cp = 1005 J/kg-K:

  C_air = 0.20 x 1.225 x 1005 = 246.2 J/K

The combined idealized heating rate is then:

  dT/dt = 1000 / (121,500 + 246.2) = 0.008214 K/s

This gives an approximately uniform-energy temperature rise of 4.928 degC
after 600 seconds. Local solid and air temperatures will differ while heat is
being redistributed, but total stored energy must still balance.


Quantities to compare
---------------------
Compare these results between Thermal Desktop and the thermal solver:

  1. Integrated heat input: exactly 1000 W.
  2. Volume-weighted average aluminum temperature versus time.
  3. Total stored-energy increase versus time.
  4. Minimum and maximum aluminum temperatures.
  5. Aluminum center temperature.
  6. Temperatures at the upper and lower aluminum faces.
  7. Total energy residual.

For an adiabatic model, the primary conservation check is:

  E(t) - E(0) = 1000 x t joules

Start by validating total power, stored energy, and volume-averaged
temperature. Only compare cell-level temperature fields after those integral
quantities agree.


Optional steady-state validation
--------------------------------
To create a steady-state benchmark, add an explicit and identical heat sink to
both models. Suitable choices include:

  - Hold all rack exterior faces at 20 degC; or
  - Apply the same external convection coefficient and ambient temperature to
    the same rack faces.

Document the selected face areas, convection coefficient, ambient temperature,
contact conductance, and radiation assumptions. Without an external heat sink,
the 1000 W model must not converge to a fixed temperature.

DETAILED BUILD PROCEDURE
========================

The exact ribbon/menu wording varies between Thermal Desktop releases. In most
releases the commands are under Thermal > FD/FEM Network, the Model Browser,
or the Case Set/Case Manager. Use the installed Thermal Desktop and
SINDA/FLUINT manuals for the labels in your release. The modeling decisions,
dimensions, units, loads, and comparison requirements below do not depend on
the interface version.


Step 1 - Freeze the validation question
---------------------------------------
Before drawing anything, record the purpose of the case. The first comparison
should answer only these questions:

  - Does each program apply exactly 1000 W?
  - Does each program conserve 1000*t joules in an adiabatic transient?
  - Does the volume-averaged aluminum temperature rise at the expected rate?
  - Do both programs use the same initial temperature and material properties?

Do not add radiation, external convection, temperature-dependent properties,
contact resistance, fans, vents, or natural-convection correlations to the
first case. Each extra mechanism makes it harder to identify the source of a
difference.

Create a short case-identification table and keep it with the results:

  Case name:                 ADIABATIC_AL_1000W
  Unit system:               SI (m, kg, s, W, J, K)
  Initial temperature:       20 degC = 293.15 K
  Rack dimensions:           0.5 x 0.5 x 1.0 m
  Aluminum origin:           (0.0, 0.0, 0.4) m
  Aluminum dimensions:       0.5 x 0.5 x 0.2 m
  Aluminum total heat:       1000 W
  Exterior boundary:         adiabatic
  Solution type:             transient
  End time:                  600 s
  Result interval:           10 s

Thermal Desktop and the thermal solver may display Celsius, but absolute
temperature inputs are often stored in kelvin internally. Confirm that an
entered 20 degC is displayed as 20 degC, not -253.15 degC or 293.15 degC.


Step 2 - Create a clean Thermal Desktop model
---------------------------------------------
1. Start a new Thermal Desktop drawing/model rather than modifying an existing
   spacecraft or electronics model. This avoids inherited symbols, radiation
   groups, contactors, heaters, and Case Set overrides.

2. Set the drawing/model unit convention to SI. Confirm that a line entered as
   0.5 measures 0.5 m in Thermal Desktop. Some AutoCAD templates use mm or
   inches even when material properties are SI.

3. Create a dedicated thermal submodel, for example VALIDATION. Put every
   node, conductor, heat load, and boundary condition for this test in that
   submodel.

4. Create Symbols for inputs if desired:

     RACK_W     = 0.5 m
     RACK_D     = 0.5 m
     RACK_H     = 1.0 m
     BLOCK_H    = 0.2 m
     BLOCK_Z    = 0.4 m
     BLOCK_Q    = 1000 W
     T_INITIAL  = 293.15 K
     T_END      = 600 s

   Symbols are optional for one case but reduce transcription mistakes when
   the mesh or boundary condition is changed later.

5. Save the untouched starting file, then save the working copy with a name
   such as ADIABATIC_AL_1000W.dwg. Do not reuse the same result database for
   materially different boundary-condition cases.


Step 3 - Decide what the rack means
-----------------------------------
The thermal-solver rack in this test is a computational domain, not a stated
sheet-metal enclosure. Therefore, do not automatically create aluminum rack
walls in Thermal Desktop. Adding wall mass or wall conduction would create a
different physical model.

For the first validation case, represent the rack boundary as six geometric
reference faces or a nonparticipating wireframe box:

  x = 0.0 m and x = 0.5 m
  y = 0.0 m and y = 0.5 m
  z = 0.0 m and z = 1.0 m

These faces locate the air/domain boundaries. They should not have thermal
capacitance, conductivity, a fixed temperature, convection, or radiation in
the adiabatic case.

Check the rack box:

  - Minimum corner is (0, 0, 0) m.
  - Maximum corner is (0.5, 0.5, 1.0) m.
  - Width is along global x.
  - Depth is along global y.
  - Height is along global z.

If Thermal Desktop displays another axis as vertical, do not rotate the
numeric model merely to make the view look conventional. The coordinates must
match the thermal-solver x/y/z convention.


Step 4 - Create the aluminum block
----------------------------------
Use a Thermal Desktop finite-difference solid or a TD Direct finite-element
solid. A parameterized FD brick is easiest for this rectangular benchmark.

1. Create a rectangular solid with minimum corner:

     x = 0.0 m
     y = 0.0 m
     z = 0.4 m

2. Set its dimensions:

     x length = 0.5 m
     y length = 0.5 m
     z length = 0.2 m

3. Verify the maximum corner is (0.5, 0.5, 0.6) m.

4. Assign it to submodel VALIDATION and give it an unmistakable name such as
   AL_BLOCK.

5. Start with a structured mesh. A useful first mesh is:

     Nx = 10
     Ny = 10
     Nz = 4

   This produces nominal 0.05 m cells in every direction and 400 solid nodes
   or elements, depending on the selected FD/FEM formulation. If the thermal
   solver uses another mesh, match its block-plane cuts as closely as possible.

6. Confirm that the generated mesh covers the complete solid and that no node
   lies outside z = 0.4 to 0.6 m.

7. Confirm the modeled solid volume from Thermal Desktop's object or mesh
   information:

     expected volume = 0.050000 m^3

   A different volume means the units, dimensions, or element thickness are
   wrong. Do not proceed until the volume matches.


Step 5 - Define and verify aluminum properties
----------------------------------------------
Create a dedicated material instead of relying on a similarly named library
entry. Name it AL_VALIDATION_CONSTANT and enter constant properties:

  Density, rho:             2700 kg/m^3
  Specific heat, cp:         900 J/kg-K
  Thermal conductivity, k:   205 W/m-K

If the TOML uses a conductivity other than 205 W/m-K, use the TOML value in
both programs. Do not use temperature-dependent aluminum data in one program
and constants in the other.

After assigning the material, verify these derived values:

  Solid volume:             0.05 m^3
  Solid mass:               135 kg
  Solid thermal capacity:   121,500 J/K

Thermal Desktop may report capacitance per node rather than one total. Sum all
AL_BLOCK nodal capacitances and verify the result is 121,500 J/K, allowing
only normal roundoff. If it is off by factors of 1000, check whether the model
uses mm while density and conductivity are entered in m-based units.


Step 6 - Apply the 1000 W heat load
-----------------------------------
1. Choose the command for a heat load applied to solids/elements, commonly
   Thermal > FD/FEM Network > Heat Load on Solids.

2. Select only AL_BLOCK. Do not select rack reference faces or air volumes.

3. Set the load type to a total/distributed heat load whose integrated value
   is 1000 W.

4. If the interface instead requests volumetric generation, enter:

     q''' = 1000 W / 0.05 m^3 = 20,000 W/m^3

   Never enter 1000 W/m^3; that would apply only 50 W to this block.

5. Make the load active from t = 0 through t = 600 s. Do not apply a ramp for
   the first case unless the thermal-solver heat source uses the same ramp.

6. Give the load a name such as Q_AL_BLOCK_1000W.

7. Use Thermal Desktop's model check, object summary, generated SINDA input,
   or heat-load report to confirm the sum of all active loads is exactly
   +1000 W. Ensure the load is not applied once per selected element as 1000 W
   per element.


Step 7 - Choose the air representation
--------------------------------------
Three levels of validation are possible. Run them in order rather than mixing
them into one first case.

Level A: aluminum-only energy check
  Do not create air thermal nodes. The block is completely adiabatic and has
  uniform internal generation. This is the cleanest check of heat, mass,
  capacitance, transient time, and units.

Level B: stationary air conduction check
  Create two air FD/FEM volumes:

    AIR_LOWER: origin (0,0,0),   size 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.4 m
    AIR_UPPER: origin (0,0,0.6), size 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.4 m

  Use exactly the thermal-solver air density, cp, and conductivity. Connect
  the z = 0.4 lower-air face to the block bottom and the z = 0.6 upper-air
  face to the block top. Use matched nodes or an ideal geometric contactor so
  no arbitrary contact resistance is introduced. This level tests conduction
  and capacity, not buoyancy.

Level C: natural convection/fluid check
  Use FloCAD or a documented fluid/convection network. Define gravity along
  global -z, use the same air properties, and document the convection model.
  A FloCAD network is a reduced-order model and should not be expected to
  reproduce the exact 3-D CFD temperature field. Compare bulk temperatures,
  energy balance, and total block-to-air heat before local cell values.

The block fills the entire x-y cross-section. Therefore AIR_LOWER and
AIR_UPPER are not connected around its sides. Do not add a flow path between
them unless the thermal-solver geometry includes a clearance or opening.


Step 8 - Apply boundary conditions
----------------------------------
For ADIABATIC_AL_1000W:

  - No fixed-temperature nodes.
  - No convection to an ambient node.
  - No radiation conductors or RadCAD calculation.
  - No contact to an unmodeled rack wall.
  - No heater controller or thermostat.

An omitted thermal connection is the adiabatic condition. Do not create a
20 degC boundary merely because the initial temperature is 20 degC. Initial
temperature and fixed boundary temperature are different concepts.

Run Thermal Desktop's model checks and inspect for unintended conductors to
boundary or arithmetic nodes. The only energy entering Level A should be the
1000 W heat load.


Step 9 - Configure the transient SINDA/FLUINT case
--------------------------------------------------
1. Create a transient Case Set/case named ADIABATIC_AL_1000W_TRANSIENT.

2. Select the transient solution option rather than steady state.

3. Set initial temperature for every AL_BLOCK node to:

     20 degC, or 293.15 K

4. Set start time to 0 s and end time to 600 s.

5. Request saved/printed results at least every 10 s. The numerical integration
   timestep and result-output interval are not the same setting.

6. For the first Level A run, choose a conservative maximum integration step,
   for example 1 s. Repeat with 0.5 s or tighter tolerances to demonstrate
   that the temperature result is insensitive to solver controls.

7. Use the normal SINDA/FLUINT transient solver and default robust convergence
   controls unless the installed manual recommends otherwise for the chosen
   FD/FEM object.

8. Disable inherited Case Set overrides that activate other heat loads,
   conductors, radiation tasks, or boundary nodes.

9. Generate the SINDA/FLUINT input and inspect the execution summary before
   accepting the run. Confirm:

     total AL_BLOCK capacitance = 121,500 J/K
     active heat input          = 1000 W
     initial temperature        = 293.15 K
     final requested time       = 600 s


Step 10 - Run and inspect solver health
---------------------------------------
Run the Thermal Desktop/SINDA case and retain the solver output file. A valid
Level A run should have no singular-network error, no uncontrolled node, no
negative capacitance, and no timestep collapse.

At selected times, the ideal aluminum-only average temperature is:

  t =   0 s: 20.0000 degC
  t =  10 s: 20.0823 degC
  t =  60 s: 20.4938 degC
  t = 300 s: 22.4691 degC
  t = 600 s: 24.9383 degC

Because the load and initial temperature are spatially uniform and every face
is adiabatic, all AL_BLOCK nodes should remain nearly identical in Level A.
Large node-to-node differences indicate a nonuniform load, unintended boundary
condition, incorrect initial temperature, or mesh/property assignment error.


Step 11 - Create result outputs
-------------------------------
Create and export the following Thermal Desktop results:

1. Volume- or capacitance-weighted average temperature of AL_BLOCK versus time.
   Do not use a simple node average if nodal volumes differ.

2. Minimum and maximum AL_BLOCK temperature versus time.

3. Center temperature near (0.25, 0.25, 0.5) m.

4. Total applied heat versus time.

5. Total stored energy or the sum of nodal capacitance times temperature
   change:

     delta E = sum_i C_i [T_i(t) - T_i(0)]

6. A CSV/table containing time, average temperature, minimum temperature,
   maximum temperature, heat input, and stored energy.

Use kelvin differences or Celsius differences consistently; a temperature
difference of 1 K equals a difference of 1 degC.


Step 12 - Export matching thermal-solver results
------------------------------------------------
From the thermal solver, collect at the same result times:

  - Integrated qdot over the block volume.
  - Volume-weighted block temperature.
  - Block minimum and maximum temperature.
  - Block-center temperature.
  - Total energy change of all modeled materials.

For cell-centered data, compute the block average as:

  T_avg = sum(T_i * V_i) / sum(V_i)

For temperature-dependent density or cp, compare energy directly rather than
assuming one constant bulk capacitance.


Step 13 - Numerical acceptance criteria
---------------------------------------
Use explicit acceptance limits so visual similarity is not mistaken for
validation. Suggested Level A criteria are:

  Geometry volume error:       <= 0.01 percent
  Applied heat error:          <= 0.01 percent
  Total capacitance error:     <= 0.01 percent
  Stored energy error at 600s: <= 0.10 percent
  Average temperature error:   <= 0.02 degC
  TD min-to-max spread:        <= 0.01 degC
  Solver min-to-max spread:    consistent with its discretization

Energy residual for either program can be reported as:

  residual = [delta E - integral(Q_in - Q_out)dt]
             / integral(abs(Q_in) + abs(Q_out))dt

For the adiabatic constant-load case, Q_out = 0 and the denominator is
1000*t.


Step 14 - Mesh and timestep convergence
---------------------------------------
After the baseline agrees, repeat at three resolutions. For example:

  Coarse block mesh:  5 x 5 x 2
  Baseline mesh:     10 x 10 x 4
  Fine block mesh:   20 x 20 x 8

For Level A, average temperature should be essentially mesh independent. For
Levels B and C, compare average and peak temperatures and the block-to-air heat
rate. Treat a localized peak as resolved only when refinement changes it by an
acceptably small amount.

Repeat the transient with smaller maximum timesteps or tighter SINDA controls.
The 600-second average temperature should remain within the selected tolerance.


Step 15 - Add a steady-state benchmark only after transient validation
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
The original sealed case cannot reach steady state. To create a second,
steady-state benchmark, define an identical sink in both programs.

Simple fixed-wall option:
  Hold selected rack boundary faces at exactly 20 degC. Record which faces are
  fixed and ensure the same geometric faces are fixed in both models.

External-convection option:
  Apply the same ambient temperature and heat-transfer coefficient to the same
  rack areas in both programs. Record:

    ambient temperature
    convection coefficient
    wetted area
    wall material and thickness, if any
    contact resistance
    radiation emissivity and surroundings, if radiation is enabled

Do not compare a Thermal Desktop fixed-temperature wall against a thermal
solver adiabatic wall or convection boundary. Those are different problems.


Troubleshooting checklist
-------------------------
If the final average is too low:
  - Verify 1000 W is total heat, not 1000 W/m^3.
  - Check for an unintended 20 degC boundary or ambient conductor.
  - Check whether radiation was left active.
  - Verify the model did not add rack-wall thermal mass.

If the final average is too high:
  - Check for a duplicated 1000 W load.
  - Verify aluminum density and cp units.
  - Confirm the block volume is 0.05 m^3.
  - Confirm temperature is not being interpreted in an incorrect unit system.

If temperatures are nonuniform in Level A:
  - Confirm every node has the same initial temperature.
  - Confirm the heat load is distributed through the solid volume.
  - Search for fixed nodes, contactors, radiation, and convection objects.

If Thermal Desktop reaches steady state in the sealed 1000 W case:
  - A heat sink or fixed node exists somewhere in the model.
  - Inspect Case Set overrides and generated SINDA conductors/boundary nodes.

If Thermal Desktop and the solver conserve energy but local fields differ:
  - Match meshes and material conductivity.
  - Match air modeling assumptions.
  - Compare contact conductance and interface placement.
  - Refine both meshes before interpreting peak temperatures.
